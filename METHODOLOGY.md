# CultureTechLens Research Methodology

**Version 1.0 — September 2026**

CultureTechLens (CTL) documents, analyzes, and preserves Black cultural knowledge through research designed to be **inspectable, source-based, and citable**.

This methodology explains how CTL conducts research, evaluates evidence, documents sources, distinguishes documented facts from interpretation, and records revisions.

## 1. Research principles

CTL research follows five principles:

- **Source-first:** Significant claims should be connected to identifiable sources whenever practical.
- **Traceability:** Readers should be able to follow a claim back to supporting evidence.
- **Context:** Sources are evaluated in historical, cultural, geographic, and institutional context.
- **Transparency:** Incomplete, disputed, conflicting, or uncertain evidence should be identified.
- **Revision:** Research can be corrected or expanded when better evidence becomes available.

## 2. Source hierarchy

CTL generally considers sources in the following order, while recognizing that the appropriate source depends on the research question:

1. Primary sources and original records
2. Official institutional records and databases
3. Scholarly research and archival collections
4. Reputable journalism and documented reporting
5. Specialized research organizations
6. Interviews, oral histories, and firsthand testimony
7. Secondary summaries and reference materials
8. Search results and discovery tools

A source's position in this list does not automatically determine its reliability. A secondary source can be useful for context or discovery, while a primary source can contain errors or require contextual interpretation.

## 3. Evidence collection

For each research project, CTL attempts to record:

- source title;
- author or organization;
- publication or creation date;
- URL or archival identifier;
- access date;
- relevant passage, data point, image, record, or other evidence;
- source type;
- relationship between the source and the claim.

Where possible, CTL preserves enough information for another researcher to locate the same evidence.

## 4. Claim classification

CTL distinguishes among:

### Documented fact
A factual proposition supported by identifiable evidence.

### Reported claim
A statement attributed to a person, organization, publication, or other source. Reporting a claim does not necessarily establish that the underlying claim is independently verified.

### Analysis
An interpretation or synthesis derived from available evidence.

### Historical interpretation
An interpretation of historical evidence where scholarly or evidentiary disagreement may exist.

### Uncertainty
A conclusion for which evidence is incomplete, conflicting, unavailable, or insufficient to establish a stronger claim.

These classifications help prevent interpretation or attribution from being presented as established fact.

## 5. Corroboration

For important claims, CTL seeks multiple sources when practical. Depending on the question, this can include:

- comparing primary and secondary sources;
- checking dates, names, and events against independent records;
- comparing multiple reports;
- reviewing archival collections;
- checking institutional databases;
- comparing original documents with later summaries.

When credible sources disagree, CTL should document the disagreement rather than silently selecting one account.

## 6. Cultural and historical context

Research involving Black history and culture considers, where relevant:

- historical period;
- geographic location;
- community context;
- terminology used during the period being studied;
- institutional power and ownership;
- archival and preservation gaps;
- differences between historical evidence and contemporary interpretation.

The purpose is to make relevant complexity visible rather than removing it from the research record.

## 7. Images and media

For visual and audiovisual materials, CTL records, when available:

- creator;
- title or description;
- date;
- collection or publication;
- source URL;
- copyright or licensing information;
- known provenance;
- relevant context.

The appearance of an image alone is not treated as proof of its date, location, identity, or historical context.

## 8. Citations

Citations should allow readers to identify the source behind a significant claim.

A general citation format is:

**Organization/Author. “Title.” Publication or Collection, date. URL. Accessed date.**

For archival material:

**Collection → Series → Item → Archive/Repository identifier.**

CTL may use different citation formats when required by a particular archive, dataset, publication, or research project.

## 9. Structured research records

Where appropriate, CTL maintains machine-readable research records containing fields such as:

```text
claim
source
source_type
author
publication_date
access_date
url
evidence
context
status
notes
```

Structured records support auditability, updates, reuse, and citation.

## 10. Version history

Research is treated as a living record. Changes may include:

- newly identified sources;
- corrected dates or names;
- updated links;
- revised interpretations;
- newly discovered evidence;
- corrections to published material.

Significant changes should be documented in the public version history.

## 11. Corrections

When CTL identifies an error, the correction should preserve the distinction between the original and corrected information when doing so improves transparency.

Corrections should not silently erase relevant research history when the history is necessary to understand how a conclusion changed.

See [CORRECTIONS.md](CORRECTIONS.md).

## 12. Artificial intelligence

AI may assist CTL with research discovery, organization, transcription, summarization, coding, data processing, or other research-support tasks.

AI-generated material is **not automatically evidence**.

Important factual claims should be checked against identifiable sources before publication. AI systems can produce incorrect, incomplete, outdated, or fabricated information, so human review and source verification remain part of the research process.

## 13. Reproducibility

Where legally and technically practical, CTL aims to publish enough information for another researcher to understand:

**What was researched → Sources used → Evidence found → Interpretation → Publication**

Sensitive information, personally identifying information, copyright-restricted material, confidential information, and restricted archival materials may not be publicly reproduced. In such cases, CTL may document the source and its provenance without publishing the restricted material itself.

## 14. Public research repository

This repository is intended to provide public access to:

- methodology;
- source lists;
- research notes;
- structured datasets;
- citation information;
- change history;
- corrections;
- supporting documentation.

The repository is a companion to the CultureTechLens website.

## 15. Citation of CTL research

When a CTL research page or repository record exists, cite the specific research work rather than the CTL homepage alone.

Recommended form:

**CultureTechLens. “Research Title.” CultureTechLens, publication date. URL.**

For repository-based research, a citation may additionally identify the GitHub repository, file path, version, release, or commit.
